Test Recovery Before You Consolidate Security

CrowdStrike’s proposed acquisition of Zscaler gives security leaders a reason to test what still works when their primary platform is unavailable.

By R Koushik, NetworkGain Consulting

NetworkGain Point of View

CrowdStrike’s proposed acquisition of Zscaler gives security leaders a reason to test what still works when their primary platform is unavailable.

A security team can remove three consoles and still have a good day at work. It can then lose access to the one console that remains and discover that it cannot answer three questions. Who still has access to a critical application? Who can change that access? Where is the record of the last change?

The proposed CrowdStrike acquisition of Zscaler gives buyers a reason to ask those questions now. It brings together two parts of the security stack that often operate separately. It also shows why buyers should judge consolidation by what their teams can do during a disruption as well as by what they can do on an ordinary day.

What the companies announced

Verified fact. On November 23, CrowdStrike announced a definitive agreement to acquire Zscaler in an all-stock transaction valued at approximately $43.9 billion. The announcement describes a proposed transaction. Customers should assess the capabilities they have today and treat plans for a combined platform as plans. CrowdStrike’s earnings release filed with the SEC

Company position. On the transaction call, CrowdStrike and Zscaler executives argued that common ownership could let them combine endpoint and network activity data more deeply and coordinate policy across more of a customer’s environment. They also argued that consolidation could simplify operations and lower costs. Asked about lock-in, CrowdStrike’s chief executive pointed to the company’s support for an open ecosystem. These statements describe management’s case for the transaction. Customers still need to see how specific controls and third-party integrations will work. Transaction call transcript filed with the SEC

NetworkGain analysis. A shared operating view can help analysts act sooner. The more important buying question is which decisions would become dependent on the same systems. A buyer can put more security work on one platform while keeping independent ways to observe an incident, limit access and recover critical services. Those options need owners, funding and a test before the buyer retires existing controls.

Count the decisions that share a dependency

Start with a critical service. For a manufacturer, that might be the system that releases production orders. For a bank, it could be the service that approves a payment batch. List the security decisions that keep that service safe during an incident. Someone must establish who has access, decide whether to suspend it, carry out that decision and preserve a record for later review.

Now ask what each decision depends on. The analyst might use one console to see device status. The access team might use another screen to revoke a session. An audit team might receive a separate report. All three could still depend on the same administrative login, the same service for distributing policy or the same store for event records. A buyer who counts screens or contracts can miss that dependency.

The reverse is also possible. A company can buy several capabilities under one agreement and keep independent controls where the operational need justifies them. It could retain a separate route for administering one critical application and keep a current copy of the access records outside the primary platform. A good assessment checks how these arrangements work in practice. The number of suppliers offers little evidence by itself.

Be precise about the type of disruption. Losing access to a management console differs from losing the controls that apply policy to live traffic. In the first case, existing rules may continue to work while administrators cannot change them. In the second case, applications may accept or reject connections in ways that depend on local configuration. Ask suppliers to describe the expected behavior of each component and test that behavior with a service owner present.

Run one exercise with three outcomes

Take one service that the business can use in a controlled test. Give the exercise a concrete starting condition. Assume the primary security console is unavailable, and an employee’s device has been reported missing. The employee still has access to the service. The team must confirm the current state, restrict that access and preserve enough evidence for an independent reviewer to reconstruct the decision.

First, check whether the responders can establish what happened. Can they obtain recent identity, access and device records through a route they can still use? Ask them to identify the age of the records and their gaps. An export from yesterday may help establish a baseline. It cannot answer whether a session started ten minutes ago. Agree in advance how much delay the use case can tolerate.

Second, check whether the responders can enforce the urgent decision. Give them an approved route that covers the affected service. They may be able to suspend the relevant account at the application or revoke access through an independent identity system. The point is to find out whether that action works when the primary platform is unavailable. Have a second person confirm that the access has ended and that other users can continue their work.

Third, check whether the responders can recover and reconcile. When the primary platform returns, does it keep the emergency restriction or restore an earlier rule? Who reviews the differences? Can the incident owner show who requested the change, who approved it, when it took effect and why it was reversed or retained? Normal service can resume before all records agree. Assign someone to resolve those differences and record the result.

Use a safe test environment or a bounded production exercise with explicit approval. Time each outcome from the moment the exercise begins. Keep the measurements simple. Record time to obtain reliable evidence, time to apply the restriction and time to reconcile both systems. Record any action that required an unavailable credential or an operator who was outside the on-call rota. Those dependencies belong in the release decision.

Give the counterargument its full weight

The strongest case for consolidating is operational. A security team can spend much of an incident finding which team owns a signal, which endpoint corresponds to an identity and which policy applied at the time. A common view can make those connections faster. It can also remove duplicated rules that have drifted apart. In a small team, a second full platform could create more administration than the team can sustain.

There is a cost to independent controls as well. An alternative access route that nobody maintains can fail at the first real use. Emergency credentials add exposure and need oversight. A second copy of sensitive event records needs retention rules, access controls and its own recovery plan. If the business cannot operate these arrangements reliably, they can make the incident harder to manage.

The appropriate response is to limit independent capability to a small set of actions the business must be able to carry out. An organisation might need to revoke a privileged session, preserve a minimum event record and restore administration for two essential services. It may accept a temporary pause in less critical work. Let the business set the tolerable delay for each action. Then fund the people and systems needed to meet it.

This principle also applies to a decision to keep several suppliers. If an organisation already relies on one identity service for all its security administrators, adding a tool may contribute little to its ability to recover. A test exposes that problem before the buyer spends money to solve the wrong one.

Make the second route an acceptance condition

Buyers often request a list of supported integrations. Ask for the result of a customer-controlled exercise as well. Select a small sample of policies and event records. Check which information the platform can export, how quickly it arrives and whether it preserves details the incident team needs. Ask the team to open and interpret those records through its alternative route during the exercise. Access to a file is only one step in recovering a useful record.

Test administrative access separately. Give the recovery role to the people who will be on call. Verify that they can use it under the stated disruption, and that each action produces a record. Limit the role to the actions the test needs. Revoke or rotate access after the exercise according to the organisation’s procedure. Repeat the test after material changes to the identity system, policy distribution or logging arrangements.

Give procurement a way to act on the result. Put the evidence requirements, supported export scope and access arrangements in the implementation acceptance plan. If a recovery test fails, identify which old capability must stay in service and for how long. Price that overlap before accepting the savings forecast. If the buyer uses a managed security provider, specify who must participate, who can approve a change and which records the customer can obtain directly.

Apply the same discipline to a future exit. Ask an operator to recreate a representative rule in another system and explain what could be lost in translation. Policy intent can often be documented in language the business understands even when implementation details differ. For example, a service owner can specify which staff may approve an order from a managed device and what happens when its status is unknown. That requirement remains useful if the implementation moves.

What could change after the proposed deal

Forward-looking inference. If the deal closes and the companies deepen integration, some customers may choose to retire separate tools earlier than they would otherwise. A package price could influence that timing. So could a stronger combined incident view. Buyers can use the same acceptance tests in either case. Run them on the capabilities available at each decision point, especially before removing an existing source of evidence or a way to change access.

A renewal may be the first practical opportunity to act. The team can decide to renew ordinary operations on the consolidated platform and retain a narrower independent capability through the next recovery exercise. That gives leaders an explicit basis for approving the savings. They can see which overlap the business still needs, who owns the test and when the next decision comes up.

The customers with the most to learn from the proposed transaction may be the ones with no plans to buy either product. Their own architecture may already group evidence, policy and administrative access behind a common dependency. A supplier announcement can prompt a review, but the findings should come from the organisation’s own systems and staff.

Decisions for leaders

  • For the security leader: Which two or three actions must the on-call team be able to take when the primary platform is unavailable? What evidence shows that it can take them within the required time?
  • For the technology leader: Which dependencies do the primary and alternative routes share? Which can the team change without adding a second full platform?
  • For procurement: What will the customer need to export, operate or recover before it retires a tool? What does it cost to retain the necessary overlap until the test passes?
  • For the board: Which services can withstand the loss of the platform, for how long, and on whose evidence? Set a review date for any exception and name the person who owns the decision.

Consolidation can improve security operations. Approve it when the team can show what becomes easier in normal use and what it can still do under pressure.
